Why is scan-shift IR margin typically kept around ~12%?

Static IR signoff usually budgets a tighter margin (often ~5-8% of Vdd) for functional max-frequency operation. Scan shift is commonly allowed a looser margin - ~10-15%, with ~12% a frequently-cited representative figure - for a few reasons:

  1. Near-worst-case simultaneous switching. During shift, most/all scan flops toggle together on the same shift clock edge - activity approaching the theoretical maximum, far above typical functional-mode activity factors. This is close to the highest instantaneous current the chip will ever draw.
  2. But not the at-speed timing corner. Shift usually runs at a reduced clock frequency and only needs correct capture/shift of flop data - it doesn't have to meet full-speed launch/capture timing the way a functional path does, so there is more real slack to absorb a deeper droop before it causes an actual failure.
  3. Cost-effective grid design. Forcing the tighter functional-mode margin onto shift would over-design the power grid/decap purely to cover an artificial all-flops-toggle pattern that only exists in shift - a looser, still-safe shift margin avoids that area/cost tax.

The exact number (12%, 10%, 15%, ...) is an empirically-tuned, company/foundry-specific signoff guideline, not a physical constant - always confirm the live value in your own methodology doc before using it.
